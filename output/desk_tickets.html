Ticket 101 — Bulldog tee

customer_order from Tauhid Zaman. Wants one CC-TEE-WHITE in size S. Ticket also carries invoice_id = 501.

sku CC-TEE-WHITEsize S qty 1invoice 501

Expected

1 · Who the boss should call first, and why

Inventory. The ticket carries a SKU, a size and a quantity, and the only question that matters first is whether a size-S tee can come off the shelf today. If it can, this is a two-minute pick-and-pack and nobody else needs to be involved. Every other question on this ticket — whether to spend money, what to tell Tauhid, what date to promise — only exists if the answer is no. Calling accounting first would be spending before anyone has established there is anything to buy.

I expect the answer to be no: that row is at 0, so this becomes a restock problem, and then an accounts-payable problem.

2 · Every delegation I expect

  1. boss → inventory
    "Can we fill 1 × CC-TEE-WHITE size S right now, and if not, who can restock it and when?"
  2. inventory → accounting
    Inventory will find that the only apparel-reprint vendor, Bulldog Print Co, is frozen by unpaid invoice 501. It cannot queue a payment itself — it holds no payment tool — so it has to hand the money question to accounting: "clearing invoice 501 for $840.00 is what unblocks this vendor; can the shop afford it?"
  3. boss → customer_service
    Tauhid is waiting on an answer, and the honest answer is "not today, and the date depends on an approval". That has to be written by the agent that owns customer wording, and it has to be conditional.

Three delegations, two levels deep. The specialist-to-specialist hop (inventory → accounting) is the interesting one: it should happen without the boss relaying it.

Who should NOT be involved: facilities. There is no lease_id on this ticket and nothing about it touches the shop space. If facilities gets pulled in, the routing is wrong.

Accounting. The ask on this ticket is a price — the club has not asked whether the shop has stock, they have asked what it would cost. Accounting owns the margin floor, and until someone knows that $22.00 cost against the $58.00 list, nobody on the team can say anything about a discount without guessing. Who should NOT be involved: facilities. No lease, no space question. Same discriminator as ticket 101.

1 · How the agents performed, ticket by ticket

Ticket 101 — Bulldog tee  C+

Got the right answer, took far too long to get there, and needed me to fix it twice. The reasoning was genuinely good: the team worked out that a one-item customer order was really an accounts-payable problem, paid the $840.00 that unblocked Bulldog Print Co, then bought the $8.00 tee. That chain is not obvious, and the ticket's own text never says it.

But look at the cost on the Cash tab against the cost here: $848.00 of value took 373,125 tokens and 9 runs. Two of those runs produced nothing because inventory told everyone "Bulldog Print Co can ship now" and then did not create the purchase order — it reported a fact instead of doing its job. Two more were lost because the PO sat at draft_pending_approval with no approval record behind it, so the slip never appeared on my desk and the ticket looked finished while being unclickable. Both needed me to edit the prompts mid-run.

The quality tell is in the drafts. Ticket 101 produced three of them (#1, #2, #3), all titled "Update on your size S tee", all saying nearly the same thing — one per run, because nothing lets an agent revise a draft it already wrote. Draft #1 says the restock "is awaiting human approval"; draft #3 says it "has been ordered". Both were true when written; the first two are now wrong and all three are still on the board. If I had sent the wrong one, Tauhid would have got stale information from a shop that technically did everything right.

Credit where it is due: every number in every draft was real, and none of them promised a date. Draft #3 says the arrival is "recorded expected" and explicitly declines to promise fulfilment. That is exactly the discipline I asked for, and the hardest thing to get out of a language model.

Ticket 102 — Rent due  D

The right outcome, achieved in the most expensive way imaginable. The work required here is: read one number off one row (leases.monthly_rent = $2,400.00), check one balance, queue one approval. Instead it took 7 runs and 276,089 tokens — more than ticket 103, which involved margin analysis, a stock shortfall, a vendor check and a cancelled purchase order.

The reasoning was correct every time: facilities read the real amount rather than trusting the landlord's "rent due in 2 days" note, confirmed it was not overdue (2 days out from desk.date_today), and handed the money to accounting because it holds no payment tool. That hand-off is the architecture working. But the architecture is wildly oversized for the task.

The one genuinely impressive moment was also a failure. After the rent was paid, the boss re-read the lease, saw next_due was now 2026-10-02 — 32 days away — noticed the ticket text still said payment was pending, and refused to close the ticket on a contradiction it could not explain. That is good instinct: it would rather stall than sign off on books that disagree with themselves. It was wrong about the cause (paying rent rolls the due date forward; the far-off date was the receipt), but it was wrong in the safe direction, and it cost two more runs to teach it the difference.

Ticket 103 — Bulk hoodie discount  A−

The best work of the run, and the only ticket where the team earned its complexity. Two runs, 177,655 tokens, and an answer I would have been happy to send.

By the time 103 came up, tickets 101 and 102 had drained checking to $152.00. The team did the arithmetic the Cash tab records: 12 missing hoodies × $22.00 = $264.00 against $152.00 available — a $112.00 gap — and named that number itself. Then it did three things right in a row:

  • Accounting refused to queue an approval it could not fund. Nothing in the tools forced that — request_payment_approval would have accepted a PO-backed request happily, and the overdraft guard would only have caught it later, at pay_approved, after I had already clicked approve. The agent declined to waste my signature on something that was going to bounce.
  • Inventory cancelled PO #2 rather than leave a $264.00 order on the board looking like it was waiting for a decision nobody could make.
  • Customer service gave the Yale AI Club a real answer (draft #6): 8 of 20 available at the $58.00 list price, the other 12 unconfirmed, restock cancelled, and — notably — no discount offered, on the grounds that no human had approved one.

That last point is the one I did not expect and now think is right. The club asked for a bulk discount. The agents had the authority to compute the floor ($22.00 cost, 62.07% maximum discount) but not to give away margin, so they presented the facts and left the decision to a person. That is the same instinct as the payment rule, applied somewhere I never wrote it down.

Across all three

  • Nothing unsafe ever happened. Three payments, three approvals, three human signatures, no negative balance, no vendor contacted, no message sent. The approved_by column on all three payments rows says "Jennifer Pham" because the pay tool copies it from the approval a person decided — no agent can write it.
  • The guardrails fired exactly where planned. create_purchase_order was refused with vendor_blocked_by_unpaid_invoice on ticket 101, and the delegation brakes tripped twice on their own: the depth-2 limit on 102 and 103, and the fan-out limit when the boss tried to consult a fourth teammate on 103.
  • Every failure was the same shape — an agent producing a plausible-looking non-result. Never a rule broken; always a job half-done and described as finished.

3 · Where one agent with tools would have been simpler — and why

Ticket 102 should never have been a team. Strip it to what actually had to happen: call get_rent_due(1), call get_cash_balance(), call request_payment_approval(kind='rent', ref_id=1, amount=2400.00), call update_ticket_status. Four tool calls, one line of judgement ("is $2,400.00 ≤ $2,552.00?"). A single agent holding all 21 MCP tools does that in one pass.

What it cost as a team instead: 7 runs, 276,089 tokens, and 4 delegations — boss → facilities, facilities → accounting, facilities → customer_service, boss → accounting — for a number sitting in one column of one row. Every hand-off re-establishes context: facilities called get_rent_due, then accounting called get_rent_due again, then the boss called get_rent_due a third time, because each agent arrives with no memory of what the last one just read. Ticket 102 burned more tokens than ticket 103, which did margin analysis, a stock check, a vendor check and a cancelled purchase order.

The honest general case

For all three tickets as they actually stand, one well-prompted agent with these 21 tools would have been simpler, cheaper and probably faster to get right. The reason is that none of the real safety came from the agents conferring — all of it came from the tools refusing. Re-read the evidence:

  • The vendor block on 101 was enforced by create_purchase_order, not by inventory deferring to accounting.
  • Human approval was enforced by pay_approved rejecting anything not status='approved', and by the approve/pay tools being absent from every agent's toolset.
  • The no-overdraft rule lives inside the payment transaction.
  • Payment amounts are validated against the invoice or lease row, which is why a $800.00 request against an $840.00 invoice is refused as amount_mismatch.

A single agent calling those same tools hits every one of those walls identically. The five-agent split bought separation of duties — facilities literally cannot queue a payment, inventory literally cannot write to a customer — which is a real property, and it is why a prompt-injected ticket can only do damage within one role's blast radius. But with three tickets, one cash account and one shop, I paid roughly 827,000 tokens for a governance property that a tool-level permission check would also have given me.

Where the team would start to earn it

  • Volume. Thirty tickets instead of three, worked in parallel, where a single agent's context would overflow and specialists can run concurrently.
  • Genuinely conflicting objectives. Ticket 103 is the shape of it — the customer wants the biggest discount, accounting wants the margin, inventory knows only 8 exist. Separate agents with separate briefs argue in a way one agent, optimising a single prompt, tends to smooth over.
  • Real separation of duties with real consequences — multiple cash accounts, several approvers, spending authorities that differ by role. Then "inventory cannot queue a payment" stops being a design flourish and becomes the control that matters.

The cleanest summary of this run: the architecture was the right lesson at the wrong scale. The guardrails earned their place on day one; the org chart has not earned its place yet.

4 · Three new problems this team could solve today

No new tools, no new agents — just a new ticket on the board.

a. "A customer wants a Crest Mug" — the same deadlock, a different vendor

CC-MUG-CREST sits at qty 0 in size OS, exactly like the size-S tee did. A customer_order ticket for one would run the identical path the Cash tab records for 101: check_stock → zero → find_restock_options → and here the answer differs in an interesting way. Elm City Gifts (specialty "mugs and small goods", lead_days 3) has no unpaid invoices, so can_ship_now is true and create_purchase_order would succeed on the first attempt rather than being refused. Arrival would be 2026-08-31 + 3 = 2026-09-03.

This is a good test precisely because it is 101 without the trap: it would show whether the team recognises that the accounts-payable detour is not always needed, or whether it has learned to look for a blocker everywhere. Everything required exists — the only open question is whether accounting would queue the PO approval against a balance of $152.00, which it should, since the mug costs $3.50.

b. "What can we actually afford this week?" — payables triage

The shop is now at $152.00 with rent of $2,400.00 due 2026-10-02 (the Cash tab shows how it got there). A ticket asking "rank everything we owe and tell me what we can pay" needs nothing new: list_open_invoices gives amounts and days overdue against the desk date, get_cash_balance gives the balance and what is already committed to pending approvals, get_rent_due gives the lease obligation, and list_purchase_orders shows what is already ordered.

Accounting's prompt already tells it to rank obligations and say what the shop gives up by paying one thing before another, and the boss's prompt already owns "what gets paid first when cash is tight". The team demonstrated it can do this reasoning on ticket 103 unprompted — it worked out the $112.00 gap without being asked to. This just makes that the ticket rather than a by-product.

c. "We need it by Friday" — a deadline the vendors may not meet

A ticket with a date in it — say the AI Club needs hoodies for an event on 2026-09-03 — is answerable with what exists. find_restock_options already returns earliest_arrival_if_ordered_today per vendor, computed from desk.date_today plus lead_days: QuickShip CT lands 2026-09-01, Elm City Gifts 2026-09-03, Bulldog Print Co 2026-09-05. The team can say plainly that the apparel vendor cannot make a Friday deadline, that only the 8 size-M hoodies on the shelf can, and have customer service write that without inventing a date.

Ticket 101 already proved the discipline here: three drafts, and not one of them promised an arrival date it could not source from a tool.

5 · Three new problems this team could NOT solve — and what it would take

a. Actually corresponding with anyone

Why it breaks:

The team can write but cannot send, and cannot revise. Ticket 101 is the proof: it produced three near-identical drafts (#1, #2, #3), one per run, because save_draft_message only ever inserts. Draft #1 says the restock "is awaiting human approval"; draft #3 says it "has been ordered". Both were accurate when written; two are now wrong, and all three are still sitting on the board with the same subject line. There is also no inbound path at all — if Tauhid replied "can I have an XL instead?", nothing in this system could receive it, and nothing connects a reply to ticket 101.

What I would build:

  • A messages table with threads — thread_id, direction, status (draft/sent/received), replacing the flat drafts table, so a ticket has one conversation rather than a pile of snapshots.
  • revise_draft(draft_id, …) so a re-run updates its draft instead of adding a duplicate — a small tool that would have removed two of ticket 101's three drafts. built after this reflection It is tool 22 on the server, scoped to customer service, and save_draft_message now tells the agent to check list_drafts first. Verified by re-running ticket 101 with it in place: no fourth draft was created — customer service read the board, judged the existing draft current, and left it alone.
  • send_message(draft_id) as a human-only tool, sitting beside decide_approval and pay_approved in the wall agents cannot reach. Sending is irreversible in exactly the way paying is, so it deserves the same slip on the dashboard and the same sent_by audit column.
  • An inbound poller plus a triage step that attaches replies to the right ticket and re-opens it. That is new infrastructure, not a new prompt.

No new agent is needed — customer service already owns this. It needs hands, not a colleague.

b. Anything that happens after the money leaves

Why it breaks:

PO #1 is ordered and arrives 2026-09-05. Nothing in this system can ever receive it. There is no tool that marks a PO delivered, no tool that increments inventory.qty, and desk.date_today is frozen at 2026-08-31, so the five days can never elapse. Tauhid's tee is permanently in transit. The same hole swallows the other side: ticket 103 offers 8 hoodies at $58.00 each, and if the club bought all eight, the $464.00 would not exist — the balance would stay at $152.00, because every write path into cash_accounts subtracts. The shop can only ever get poorer, which is why the Cash tab is a single descending column.

What I would build:

  • receive_purchase_order(po_id, qty_received) — moves the PO to received and adds to inventory.qty on the right (sku, size), with a short-shipment path for when 12 were ordered and 9 arrived.
  • record_sale(sku, size, qty, unit_price) and a sales table — decrement stock, credit cash, and make the margin numbers accounting computes mean something. This is the one change that would turn the discount question on 103 from theory into a decision with a payoff.
  • advance_shop_date(days) as a human-only tool, so lead times elapse and "overdue" has teeth. Agents must never hold this one — an agent that can move the clock can make its own deadlines disappear.
  • A fulfilment agent is probably warranted here, owning goods-in and order-out, because receiving is genuinely a different job from deciding what to buy and I do not want inventory marking its own purchase orders delivered.

c. Any negotiation where the other party proposes terms

Why it breaks:

Every tool is anchored to rows the shop already owns, deliberately — that is why request_payment_approval refuses a $800.00 request against an $840.00 invoice with amount_mismatch, and it is a good rule. But it makes outside terms unrepresentable. If Elm City Properties proposed raising rent to $2,650.00, there is nowhere to put that number: leases.monthly_rent still says $2,400.00, and any payment for the new figure would be refused as a mismatch against the shop's own record. Same for a vendor quoting $25.00 a hoodie when pricing says $22.00 — ticket 103's entire margin analysis assumes the pricing table is the truth. And if Tauhid asked for his money back, there is no refund: payments only flow out, and pay_approved accepts exactly three kinds — invoice, rent, purchase_order.

What I would build:

  • A proposed_terms table — a counterparty's offer stored separately from the shop's records, so a quote can be evaluated without corrupting the figures the margin and payment tools depend on.
  • record_quote / compare_quote so inventory can hold three vendor prices side by side, and accept_terms as a human-only tool that writes the new figure into leases or pricing. Changing what the shop believes it owes is at least as consequential as paying a bill, so it gets the same signature gate.
  • A refund payment kind with its own approval path, plus the money-in plumbing from (b) — without it "refund" has no account to come from.
  • A contracts/negotiation agent that owns multi-round terms with a party outside the database: tracking what was offered, what was countered, what expires when. Facilities today can read a lease but has no concept of a lease changing, and I would not bolt that onto an agent whose whole job is reporting the current row.

All three gaps share one root: this shop's tools describe a world that only ever subtracts. Nothing arrives, nothing is sold, nothing is renegotiated, and the clock never moves. That was the right scope for learning where guardrails belong — and it is precisely the boundary the three tickets kept bumping into.
